# Government Survey Method in Land Surveying

<!-- The **Government Survey method** was adopted in 1785. This method played an important role as the United States expanded westward. You might also hear this called the *geodetic* or *rectangular survey system*. This system is used in more than 30 states, mainly in the Midwest. -->

The **Government Survey method** was adopted in 1785. This method supported United States westward expansion. This system is also called the *geodetic* or *rectangular survey system*. The system is used in more than 30 states, mainly in the Midwest.

The system uses imaginary lines that run across the landscape. These lines run north to south and east to west. When they intersect, they create a checkerboard pattern. These intersections are then broken down into smaller units. This system works well for identifying larger parcels of land. However, it's not the best choice for smaller or irregularly shaped lots.

## Principal Meridians and Base Lines

<!-- - **Principal Meridians** are a series of numbered imaginary lines that run from north to south across the country. If you're using the Government Survey method to survey land, these principal meridians are essential reference points. -->

- **Principal Meridians** are a series of numbered imaginary lines that run from north to south across the country. These principal meridians serve as reference points in the Government Survey method.

- **Base Lines** are another set of imaginary lines running east-west. They intersect with the principal meridians. This creates a web of reference points across the landscape.

## The Grid System: Checks, Townships, and Sections

The grid system formed by the principal meridians and base lines can be broken down as follows:

1. **Checks**: Large blocks measuring 24 miles on each side.
2. **Townships**: Within each check, townships are 6 miles on each side.
3. **Sections**: Each township contains sections, each being 640 acres or 1 square mile.

## Township Location and Structure

A township is defined at the intersection of **township tiers** and **range lines**:

- **Township tiers** run east-west and are parallel to base lines.
- **Range lines** run north-south and are parallel to principal meridians.

The location of a township is identified by its distance north or south of base lines and east or west of principal meridians.

Within each township:

- There are 36 numbered sections, starting with section number 1 in the northeast corner.
- Sections can be further divided into halves, quarters, and smaller parcels.

## Survey Monuments

Each section corner is marked by a **survey monument**. This is a physical marker that marks the grid system on the actual ground.
