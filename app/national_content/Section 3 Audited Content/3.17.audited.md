# Testate vs. Intestate Succession in Property Ownership

<!-- Imagine you're learning about how **property** and **estates** are handled after someone passes away. This is crucial for understanding how ownership transitions, especially in cases where someone hasn't left a will. So, let's dive into two key concepts: **testate** vs. **intestate succession**. -->

## Testate Succession

When someone passes away and they've set up a **will** or **living trust**, they've died _testate_. This means they've made clear their wishes for how their **estate**, including property and other assets, should be handed over.

## Intestate Succession

Now, consider someone didn't leave a will or trust; they've died _intestate_. One might be wondering, "Who gets the estate then?" That's where state laws come into play, specifically the **laws of descent and distribution**, often referred to as **intestate succession** statutes. These laws outline a hierarchy of relatives — **heirs** — who are eligible to inherit the estate.

### Role of Executor and Administrator

- When there is a will, an **executor** is named to distribute the estate according to the deceased's wishes.
- Without a will, no executor is named, so a court appoints an **administrator**.
- The administrator's job is similar: settling debts and distributing the estate to the rightful heirs as defined by law.

### What Happens if No Heirs Are Found?

If there is absolutely no one who qualifies as an heir under the intestate succession laws, the estate, including any **real estate**, does not remain ownerless. Instead, it **escheats** — meaning it reverts back to the state. Essentially, if no heirs are found, the state becomes the default inheritor.
