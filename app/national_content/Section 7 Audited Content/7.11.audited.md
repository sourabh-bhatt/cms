# Assumable and Non-Assumable Mortgages

**Assumable** and **non-assumable mortgages** differ in how loans can be transferred between parties, which affects real estate transactions.

## What is an Assumable Mortgage?

An assumable mortgage allows a buyer to take over, or "assume," the remaining balance of a seller's mortgage. Example: A seller has an outstanding loan with a **low interest rate**, far below current market rates. By assuming this mortgage, the buyer steps into the seller's shoes, continuing to benefit from these lower rates.

However, assuming a mortgage also means taking on the full **responsibility for paying back the entire debt**. The buyer assumes both the benefits and the **liability**.

## Non-Assumable Mortgages

Not all mortgages can be assumed. Some mortgage agreements prohibit the transfer of the loan to another party. These are known as **non-assumable mortgages** - loans that cannot be transferred to another party.

## The Role of Lender Approval and the Assumption Clause

Even if a mortgage is assumable, the process is not automatic:

- The **lender must approve** the assumption.
- This approval follows specific guidelines and conditions outlined in the mortgage agreement.
- These conditions are often detailed in the **assumption clause**, which explains whether and under what terms a mortgage can be assumed.

## Key Points

The ability to assume a mortgage depends on both the lender's approval and the terms of the original mortgage agreement.
