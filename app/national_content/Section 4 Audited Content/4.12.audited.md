# Forfeiture of Contract in Real Estate Transactions

<!-- When dealing with real estate contracts, there's an important concept you need to understand called the **forfeiture of contract**. -->
The **forfeiture of contract** occurs when **buyers** purchase property from **sellers** using **land installment contracts**, also called **contracts for deed**. These contracts are a way to finance real estate purchases.

## Foreclosure vs. Forfeiture

When a buyer defaults on the contract, there are two primary legal avenues depending on state laws:

### Foreclosure

- Foreclosure is an option based on state foreclosure laws.
- The installment contract creates an **equitable vendor's lien**, which is an implied lien for the unpaid purchase price.
- This lien exists when the seller has no other security beyond the buyer's promise to pay.

### Forfeiture

- In some states, **forfeiture** allows the seller to regain possession by canceling the buyer's rights under the contract.
- This requires the contract to include a **forfeiture clause**.
- If forfeiture is enforced:
  - The buyer loses their rights in the property.
  - The title shifts back to the seller.
- The buyer can remedy the breach and cover the seller's costs for issuing the notice of forfeiture to avoid losing the property.

## Issuing a Notice of Forfeiture

Issuing a notice of forfeiture requires strict adherence to the contract terms and relevant state laws. Key requirements include:

- Clearly detailing the exact property involved.
- Specifying how the buyer violated the contract terms.
- Stating the amount owed clearly.
- Informing the buyer of the period to fix the breach (often 30 days).
- Following the required methods for delivering or publishing the notice.
