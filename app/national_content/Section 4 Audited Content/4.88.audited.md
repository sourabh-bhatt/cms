# 4.88 Understanding Incompetency and Contract Capacity

Incompetency affects the **validity of a contract** based on an individual's **capacity** to enter into that contract. The law assumes all adults can make contracts. However, there are specific exceptions where an individual's incapacity to contract renders the contract either **void** or **voidable**.

## Lack of Capacity: Void Contracts

Some individuals lack the legal **capacity** to contract, making any contract entered into by such individuals **void**. This primarily includes individuals who have been declared mentally incompetent by a court. In simple terms, if someone has been declared by a court as mentally incompetent, any contract they enter into is considered **void from the start**.

## Limited Capacity: Voidable Contracts

Some people have limited capacity to make contracts. In these cases, the contracts are not automatically null; instead, they are **voidable**. This means that specific actions must be taken to declare the contract not binding if one party wishes to avoid their responsibilities under the contract.

### Examples of Individuals with Limited Capacity

-   **Minors**
-   **Intoxicated persons**

The law provides a level of protection for these individuals, acknowledging that while they can enter contracts, they might not fully understand or intend the implications of their agreements.

## Contract Enforceability Assessment

When assessing contract enforceability, determining whether any party had **full**, **limited**, or **no capacity** at the time of the agreement becomes necessary. In cases of limited or no capacity, the contract may be **voidable** or **void**, offering a pathway to contest or invalidate the agreement if necessary.

These principles enable identification of contracts that may not be legally binding due to issues of **competency**.
